Payments guide

How to collect tuition and payments from co-op families without chasing them

By Microschool Ledger November 2026 8 min read

Most small schools don't have a collections problem; they have a clarity problem. Families pay late when they aren't sure what they owe, when it's due, or how to pay. This guide covers the payment methods small schools use, how to set up payment plans and due dates, a sample payment policy you can adapt, and how to remind families without making it awkward.

Choosing how families can pay

Offer two or three ways to pay, not six. Every method you accept is another place you have to look when you check who has paid.

MethodGood forWatch out for
Check or cashFamilies who prefer it; no fees.Someone has to deposit it, and record it. Cash needs a written receipt.
Venmo, Zelle or similar appsSmall, quick payments like field trips.Payments arrive with a note you have to match to a family by hand. Check the app's rules for business use.
Card or bank transfer (ACH) through a payment processorTuition, autopay, and families who want to pay from a link.Processing fees. Bank transfers usually cost less than cards but take a few days to clear.
AutopayMonthly tuition on a payment plan; the most reliable way to get paid on time.Families need to know the amount and date in advance, and how to change or stop it.
Card fees

Decide up front whether the school absorbs card fees or passes a fee on to families who pay by card. If you pass it on, say so before they pay, offer a no-fee option such as bank transfer, and check your processor's rules and your state's law.

Payment plans, deposits and due dates

A payment plan turns one large annual tuition figure into amounts families can plan around. The most common setup in small schools:

  • A deposit to hold the spot, often a set percentage of annual tuition, due when the family enrolls.
  • The rest split into monthly payments over 10 or 12 months, each due on the same day of the month.
  • Sibling discounts applied before the split, so each monthly amount is already right.
  • The last payment absorbs any rounding, so the total always matches annual tuition exactly.

For example, $6,000 tuition with a 20% deposit and a 10-month plan is a $1,200 deposit and ten payments of $480. Write both the schedule and the total into the family's enrollment agreement, so there's one answer to "how much do we owe?"

Extra charges, like field trips, supplies or a materials fee, should go on the same family account. Families would much rather see one balance than three separate requests.

A sample payment policy

Put your payment rules in writing once, share them at enrollment, and point back to them when questions come up. Adapt this to your school:

Tuition and payment policy

  1. A deposit of [amount] holds your child's place and is due at enrollment. [Say whether the deposit is refundable, and until when.]
  2. The remaining tuition is paid in [10 / 12] monthly payments of [amount], due on the [1st] of each month from [first month] to [last month].
  3. We accept [bank transfer, card, check]. Card payments include a [fee / no fee].
  4. Families can enroll in autopay. Autopay payments are taken on the due date, and we'll tell you before an amount changes.
  5. If a payment is more than [number] days late, we'll contact you to agree a plan. [Describe any late fee here, or say there is none.]
  6. Scholarship payments are credited to your account when the school receives them. Any part of tuition the scholarship doesn't cover remains the family's responsibility.
  7. If your child withdraws, [describe what is owed and any refund].
  8. Questions about your balance? Email [address]; we'll reply within [two school days].

This is a starting point, not legal advice. Have someone familiar with your state's rules review your final policy and enrollment agreement.

Reminders that don't feel awkward

Reminders work when they're expected, specific and easy to act on:

  • Send them on a schedule, not when you get frustrated. A reminder a few days before the due date reads as helpful; one sent out of the blue reads as a complaint.
  • Include the exact amount and what it's for: "$480 for October tuition," not "your balance."
  • Include a way to pay right there, such as a payment link, so the reminder can be finished in one step.
  • Send it to every parent on file. The one who handles the bills may not be the one on your main contact list.
  • Check before you send. A reminder sent to a family that already paid does more harm than one sent late.
  • Keep the tone neutral. Most late payments are forgotten, not refused.

When a scholarship pays part of the bill

Scholarship money changes the question from "has the family paid?" to "how much is left for the family to pay?" Two habits prevent most confusion:

  • Count scholarship money when it arrives, not when it's expected. A balance that assumes a payment that hasn't come yet will be wrong when it's late.
  • Match each scholarship payment to the student it covers, so siblings' balances stay separate and correct.

Keeping track of who has paid

Whatever you use, every payment needs to answer four questions: which family, how much, when, and what it paid for. A spreadsheet can do this for a handful of families. It gets harder when payments come in through several methods, siblings share an account, or a scholarship covers part of the bill.

Signs you've outgrown the spreadsheet:

  • You rebuild a family's balance by hand when a parent asks.
  • Payments arrive from a processor, an app and the mail, and you match each one yourself.
  • You aren't sure a reminder is right until you check three places.
  • Scholarship deposits arrive without telling you which student they're for.
